Axes of Loxodromic Elements in the Picard Group

Enter a matrix [[a,b],[c,d]] with ad − bc = 1, representing an element of PSL(2, ℂ). If it is loxodromic, the tool draws its axis — the geodesic in H³ = {(z,t) : z ∈ ℂ, t > 0} joining its two fixed points on ∂H³ = ℂ ∪ {∞} — and, when the entries are Gaussian integers, the closed geodesic that the axis induces on the quotient orbifold H³/PSL(2, ℤ[i]). Entries accept Gaussian integers and general complex numbers: 2, i, -i, 1+i, 3-2i, 0.5+1.25i.

Entering your own matrix. Type in the four boxes directly — the picture redraws as you type, and the determinant is shown live underneath. Since ad − bc = 1 is a constraint, editing one entry will usually break it; type any three entries and press the matching solve for button to have the fourth computed, exactly in ℤ[i]. Solving for b with c = 1 always succeeds, since then b = ad − 1. If no Gaussian-integer value exists — for example a = d = 2, c = 5, where 5 ∤ 3 in ℤ[i] — the tool says so rather than rounding.

What is being drawn

For A = [[a,b],[c,d]] ∈ SL(2,ℂ) with c ≠ 0, the fixed points on ∂H³ are the roots of cz² + (d−a)z − b = 0, so their sum is the invariant fA = (a−d)/c used in the paper. The axis of a loxodromic A is the geodesic joining those two roots: in the upper half-space model it is the semicircle orthogonal to ℂ with the two fixed points as endpoints. A acts on its axis as a translation by ℓ = 2 log|λ| together with a rotation by θ = 2 arg λ, where λ is the eigenvalue of modulus > 1; the complex number L = ℓ + iθ is the complex translation length, and tr A = ±2 cosh(L/2).

For A ∈ P = PSL(2, ℤ[i]), the image of the axis in H³/P is a closed geodesic of length ℓ. The lower view shows it: one fundamental segment [x₀, A·x₀] of the axis is sampled at unit speed and each sample is pulled back into the Bianchi fundamental domain F = {(z,t) : |Re z| ≤ ½, 0 ≤ Im z ≤ ½, |z|² + t² ≥ 1} by the usual reduction (translate by a Gaussian integer, invert through the unit hemisphere by z ↦ −1/z, repeat, then fold by z ↦ −z). The geodesic therefore appears as several arcs inside F, coloured from start to end, whose endpoints match up under the face pairings.

When A is reversible the reversing involution maps the axis to itself, reversing its orientation, so the stabiliser of the axis in P is infinite dihedral rather than infinite cyclic. The image of the axis in the orbifold is then a mirror geodesic: a segment run back and forth rather than an embedded circle — the three-dimensional analogue of Sarnak's reciprocal geodesics through the order-two cone points of the modular surface. In the lower view this shows up as arcs that retrace one another. On the faces Im z = 0 and Im z = ½, where z ↦ −z identifies (x, 0) with (−x, 0), the two passes appear instead as mirror images in the plane Re z = 0, since both points are legitimate representatives of the same orbit. The tool therefore reports the mirror property from the algebraic test below, not from the picture.

The reversibility panel implements the classification of Reversibility and its asymptotic counting in the Picard group (D. Das, K. Gongopadhyay, A. Mukhopadhyay): a loxodromic g ∈ P is reversible (equivalently, by Proposition 3.1, strongly reversible) precisely when it is conjugate to a matrix in the special normal form [[d + ck, b],[c, d]], that is when fg ∈ {0, 1, i, 1+i} (Lemma 4.2, Theorem 1.1); each reversible loxodromic conjugacy class contains exactly eight such special representatives (Lemma 5.2). The search button walks a ball in P in the word metric on S, T, T_i and reports the special representatives γAγ⁻¹ it finds.
